Why the Circular Economy Makes Sense for Student Life

September 15, 2026 · by Ashish Bommera · 5 min read

If you've ever walked past a hostel or dorm building on move-out day, you've seen it: mattress toppers, study lamps, storage bins, and furniture still in good condition, stacked next to a dumpster because nobody had the time, transport, or reason to keep it. This happens on almost every campus, almost every semester. It's not because students don't care — it's because the default model, buy-and-discard, was never actually built for how student life works.

The mismatch

The "circular economy" is a simple idea: instead of a straight line from raw material → product → landfill, keep products in use, in circulation, for as long as possible — through reuse, sharing, and refurbishing, rather than one owner buying it new and throwing it away.

Campus life is one of the clearest places this makes obvious sense, because the underlying pattern repeats every single semester: thousands of people moving in and out of the same buildings, needing the same basic set of things, for the same fixed period of time, over and over. That's not a use case buying-to-discard was designed for — it's almost exactly the use case shared, reused inventory was designed for.

What buy-and-discard actually costs

  • Manufacturing waste. Every item bought new and discarded after one semester used real raw materials and energy for a few months of use.
  • Landfill volume. Furniture that's still functional but not worth the hassle of moving or reselling overwhelmingly ends up thrown out, not donated.
  • Repeated manufacturing for the same demand. The same category of item — a basic study lamp, a mattress topper — gets manufactured fresh every year for a need that hasn't actually changed.

What a shared model looks like instead

One insured bundle of furniture, used by one student for a semester, collected, checked, and issued to the next student the following term — the same physical items serving multiple students across multiple years, instead of one item per student per semester ending up in a dumpster.

This isn't a sacrifice or a downgrade. For a student, it's the same or better furniture, delivered and collected instead of self-managed, at a lower total cost than buying new every time. The environmental benefit isn't something the student has to trade convenience for — it's a side effect of a model that was already the more practical choice.

The most sustainable version of a mattress topper isn't a "green" one — it's the one that gets used by five students instead of thrown out after one.

Why this matters beyond one dumpster

Multiply one hostel building's move-out day by every hostel, every campus, every semester, and the scale is significant — even though each individual instance looks small. Campuses are, structurally, one of the best-suited environments for a circular, shared-use model to work at scale: high turnover, predictable timing, and genuinely repeatable demand.

What this looks like in practice
